Research paper review: Multimodal AI Teaching Copilot
I have examined your seven-page paper, including the methodology, equations, system architecture, screenshots, tables, latency graph, experimental results, discussion, conclusion, and references.
My assessment is that you have a promising implementation/research paper with substantial engineering work behind it, but I would not submit this version to a rigorous IEEE journal yet.
The main reason is not that you need to make the paper longer or add more complex AI models. It is that the paper describes your system in considerable detail, but its experiments do not yet establish how well the most important parts of the system actually perform. That distinction matters. A system can be impressive and fully implemented while its research paper still needs stronger evidence.
My overall assessment
Strong: Implementation and system design
The architecture, deterministic slide planner, LLM safeguards, teacher controls, equations, and component details make this more substantial than a basic project-description paper.

Promising: Measured system performance
You report six live lectures totaling about 114 minutes, 163 generated slides, 765 transcribed utterances, latency distributions, image-retrieval measurements, and software tests. These are useful engineering results.

Weakest: Validation of research claims
There is no baseline comparison, no ground-truth evaluation of topic segmentation, no systematic expert assessment of slide quality, and no user study. These gaps matter more than adding pages.

Needs fixing: Submission readiness
There is a visible figure/table overlap in the rendered document, several evaluation details need clarification, and the related work needs to engage with newer, closely related research.

Your existing results are not worthless or inherently inadequate. They demonstrate that you have built and tested an end-to-end system. The issue is that they currently establish feasibility more strongly than effectiveness. IEEE_Paper_Multimodal_AI_Teaching_Copilot.docx

2. The five major weaknesses to address
These are the issues I would prioritize before changing the wording or adding more diagrams.
Weakness 1: You show system performance, but not enough system quality
Your current results tell us how many utterances, interpretation units and slides the system processed, together with the time taken by two components.
But consider the difference between these two statements:
What your current measurements establish:
The system processed live lectures and generated slides with measured transcription and LLM latencies.

What a reviewer still needs evidence to establish:
The system identifies lecture topics correctly, creates relevant and factually reliable slides, avoids unnecessary transitions, and improves on an alternative approach.

The second statement is much closer to the central research claims of your paper.
At present, you do not give a systematic quantitative evaluation of:
- Whether detected topic boundaries match the actual lecture structure.
- Whether generated slides contain the important facts from each spoken segment.
- Whether slides contain unsupported or incorrect claims.
- Whether slide changes are stable and appropriate.
- Whether retrieved images are relevant across the full set of search attempts.
My recommendation: Keep your present performance measurements, but add experiments that evaluate the quality of the output.
Weakness 2: There is no baseline comparison
This is probably the most important research-design weakness.
Your paper argues that using a bounded LLM interpreter with deterministic slide-planning rules produces a stable, continuity-aware presentation. However, you do not compare this design against a simpler alternative. IEEE_Paper_Multimodal_AI_Teaching_Copilot.docx
IEEE_Paper_Multimodal_AI_Teaching_Copilot.docx

A reviewer could reasonably ask:
How do we know the deterministic planner improves anything compared with asking an LLM to generate or update slides directly?

You do not need to invent a new model to answer this question. You can use recorded lectures to compare approaches.
I recommend this experiment:
Approach	What it tests
Baseline A: LLM-based slide updates without your confirmation/dwell rules	Whether your slide-planning rules improve stability
Proposed system	Your complete implementation
Ablation B: Proposed system with selected planning rules disabled	Which parts of your approach contribute to the observed results
Use the same recordings and comparable inputs wherever possible. Record the number of unnecessary slide changes, topic-transition errors, key facts omitted and end-to-end latency.
The ablation experiment is particularly valuable because your technical argument is about the benefits of separating LLM interpretation from deterministic slide control.
Do not claim that your planner is superior until you have measured the difference.
Weakness 3: Your six lectures are a good feasibility test, but a limited evaluation dataset
Your six sessions cover several academic subjects and total approximately 114 minutes. That is a useful starting point.
However, the paper says these lectures were spoken by the authors during development. IEEE_Paper_Multimodal_AI_Teaching_Copilot.docx

That creates two limitations.
First, the test conditions may not represent ordinary classrooms, where speaking speeds, accents, background noise, lecture organization and terminology vary.
Second, if parameters were adjusted after observing these sessions, the same sessions cannot independently demonstrate how well those choices generalize to unseen lectures.
My recommendation: Collect additional held-out lecture recordings that were not used to tune the system. Include different speakers and lecture styles, and describe the conditions. Even a modest, carefully selected independent test set is more useful than simply processing the existing recordings again.
You do not need to claim that six lectures are invalid. You need to describe them as preliminary system evaluation and provide stronger evidence for generalization.
Weakness 4: Your literature review needs closer, newer comparisons
Your Related Work section has a sensible foundation: multimedia-learning theory, Whisper, sentence embeddings, slide generation and LLMs in education. Your existing comparison table also gives readers a useful overview of different approaches. IEEE_Paper_Multimodal_AI_Teaching_Copilot.docx

A reviewer may ask:

You do not need to conduct an exhaustive sensitivity analysis of every number. Select the few parameters most responsible for real-time responsiveness and display stability.

4. Exactly which additional experiments should you perform?
Here is the experiment plan I would recommend, in priority order.

Experiment 1 — Baseline and ablation comparison
Highest priority
Compare your proposed system with a simpler LLM-based slide-update method. Then disable one or two important planning safeguards in a controlled replay.
Measure: Unnecessary slide changes, topic-boundary mistakes, omitted key facts and end-to-end delay.
Why: This is the most direct way to establish whether your central architectural decisions provide a measurable benefit.




Experiment 2 — Evaluate slide content and lecture structure
Highest priority
Take a subset of unseen recordings and create a reference annotation of important lecture facts and topic boundaries. Have at least two independent evaluators assess generated content using a clear rubric.
Measure: Topic-boundary precision/recall or F1, key-fact coverage, factual error rate, relevance and readability.
Why: Your paper claims that the system interprets lectures and converts them into coherent educational slides. These measures test that claim directly.




Experiment 3 — End-to-end latency and stability
High priority
Measure the time from the end of a relevant spoken segment until the corresponding slide update is visible on the display. Include buffering, queueing, the LLM call, composition, rendering and delivery.
Measure: Median, 95th-percentile and maximum observed delay, plus unnecessary updates per minute.
Why: Your present graph measures speech-to-text and LLM-call latency separately, while your detailed discussion reports that a line sometimes waited a median of 11 seconds before submission. The complete response time is more representative of the live system's behavior. IEEE_Paper_Multimodal_AI_Teaching_Copilot.docx





Experiment 4 — Image retrieval evaluation
Your paper reports 33 accepted images from 63 searches in the live-lecture table. Separately, it reports that the first returned image was relevant for all 28 concepts that received an image in a 30-concept labelled test set. IEEE_Paper_Multimodal_AI_Teaching_Copilot.docx

These are two different measurements, and the paper should explain the distinction clearly.
Report retrieval coverage (whether a suitable image was returned), relevance among retrieved images, and the human evaluation criterion. Do not count only successful searches when calculating coverage.
Why: This will clarify both the strength and the limitation of your CLIP-based retrieval process.



You do not need a completely new benchmark for every component. A carefully annotated subset, a transparent baseline comparison, and reproducible measurements would already be meaningful improvements.

10. Will your paper be accepted?
Nobody can responsibly guarantee acceptance from a draft review. The decision depends on the actual journal's scope, the novelty relative to its literature, the experimental evidence and the reviewers' assessments.
My submission recommendation is:
- As a technical implementation paper: promising, with meaningful detail and credible preliminary measurements.
- As a paper for a rigorous IEEE journal right now: not ready. I would expect concerns about insufficient validation, lack of baseline comparison and unclear differentiation from related work.
- After targeted improvements: potentially a much stronger submission, provided the results support the claims and the selected journal is a good fit.
For a journal specifically focused on learning technologies, your current absence of a user study may become an additional concern. That does not mean every journal requires a learning-outcome experiment; it means you must choose a venue whose research expectations match the contribution you can substantiate.
