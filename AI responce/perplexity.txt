1. Verdict
Partly. You can build a live, dynamically composed stick-figure theatre under these constraints. You cannot honestly promise “any spoken story → a correct, immersive animated scene within 2–3 seconds” with no substantial limitations.

The achievable product is:

A persistent 2D world with a finite vocabulary of executable actions and object capabilities, composed into new scenes from unrestricted language.

That is not the same as a fixed collection of story templates. Newton, a boy, or a fictional traveller can all use the same sitting, holding, walking, and riding mechanics in previously unseen combinations.

But there is an unavoidable boundary:

Open-ended language input is feasible. Open-ended visual understanding, object coverage, and animation behaviour are not solved by adding a scene graph.

If “not template based” also means “no finite library of action implementations, rig types, or object affordances,” then my answer is no.

My engineering assessment:

Requirement	Assessment
Draw and animate a few simple 2D characters smoothly	Feasible; not the main risk
Compose unseen stories from reusable mechanics	Feasible within supported mechanics
Correct sitting, holding, riding, and falling contacts	Feasible with explicit anchors and constraints
Recognisable curated Newton/Einstein/Curie	Feasible with offline artwork
Arbitrary unfamiliar objects with automatically correct interactions	Not reliable
Arbitrary verbs becoming appropriate animation	Not reliable
Hand-animated YouTube storytelling quality, generated live	Not a realistic promise
Guaranteed ≤3 seconds using the free Groq service	Not a defensible guarantee
Useful classroom demonstration in 1–2 days	Feasible if narrowly scoped
These are engineering judgments, not results from a benchmark of your implementation.

2. The real hard problem: executable meaning, not rendering
Your diagnosis is largely correct. More precisely, the hard problem is turning language into a persistent, temporally coherent world whose relations are actually enforced.

Your previous puppet experiment failed at this boundary. A better parser would help, but “rider on horse” in JSON still does not put the rider on the horse. You need an execution system that makes the relation true.

Use three representations, not one
I would separate:

Semantic world state: who exists, identity, location, possession, posture, and persistent relations.

Event plan: what changes, in what order, with what preconditions and completion conditions.

Render state: coordinates, joint angles, layers, paths, and camera transforms computed by code.

The LLM writes only the first two. It never writes coordinates or rendering code.

For example:

json
{
  "base_revision": 12,
  "entities_added": [
    {
      "id": "apple_1",
      "kind": "apple"
    }
  ],
  "events": [
    {
      "type": "fall",
      "actor": "apple_1",
      "source": {
        "entity": "tree_1",
        "region": "canopy"
      },
      "destination": {
        "entity": "newton",
        "region": "head"
      },
      "completion": "contact"
    }
  ]
}
That describes the intended event. Your compiler decides where in the canopy the apple starts, whether a downward path can reach the head, how long it takes, and how impact is drawn.

The previous line’s sitting and under relations remain active unless explicitly changed.

Relations must compile into constraints
These are suggested mechanics, not claims about an existing turnkey library:

Semantic relation	Deterministic implementation
Sitting on chair	Pelvis at seat anchor; feet and knees posed within joint limits
Sitting under tree	Ground-sitting pose within canopy’s horizontal region, clear of trunk
Holding flask	Prop grip anchor attached to hand; wrist and elbow solved around it
Riding horse	Pelvis attached to saddle; rider moves with horse; legs layered around body
Inside shed	Occupant placed within interior region; shed uses cutaway/front-wall policy
Bird in sky	Assigned aerial region rather than ground baseline
Falling onto head	Target-contact trajectory with impact event
Speaking to friend	Speaker-bound bubble, head orientation, optional pointing gesture
Two-bone IK is useful, but it does not choose the correct seat, determine what is inside a shed, or decide whether an action is physically possible. Those are capability and planning problems.

For simple stick figures, implement analytic arm/leg IK yourself. You probably do not need a general-purpose constraint solver initially.

Replace the open verb list with a closed executable vocabulary
Keep the original verb in metadata, but execute only supported operators:

text
move, change_posture, attach, detach, look_at,
point_at, speak, fall, follow_path, enter, exit
Compound behaviours such as ride can compile into several operations.

An unsupported verb must produce an explicit fallback, not a confident but unrelated gesture. “He forged an alliance” must not become a hammering animation merely because forge matches a physical action.

Also separate explicit content from embellishment. “The apple hit him” does not necessarily assert that he looked surprised. You can add that as an approved stylistic convention, but do not let such embellishments silently become factual story state.

Persistent identity and scheduling matter as much as pose
You also need:

Stable entity IDs across sentences and pronouns.

Revision numbers to reject stale LLM responses.

Explicit scene changes and time jumps.

An event queue with interruptibility.

A policy for contradictory or impossible instructions.

A distinction between foreground events and background loops.

Otherwise, the correct apple may hit a newly spawned Newton while the original Newton remains elsewhere.

A further constraint: ≤3 seconds to begin an animation is not ≤3 seconds to finish it. If each sentence creates six seconds of sequential action, the display will fall progressively behind. Shorten, overlap, interrupt, or summarise events rather than allowing an unbounded queue.

3. Recommended stack and asset strategy
My concrete choice
text
Existing Whisper pipeline
        ↓
One Groq call: sentence + compact world state + capability vocabulary
        ↓
Schema validation + entity/reference/capability checks
        ↓
Persistent semantic world + event compiler
        ↓
Anchor placement + simple IK + event scheduler
        ↓
PixiJS browser renderer
Use TypeScript for the world model, compiler, scheduler, and renderer. Keep the existing Python service for Whisper and your physics teaching tools; do not introduce a Python round trip for every animation frame.

PixiJS is MIT-licensed and provides WebGL/WebGPU rendering. It is a suitable renderer, not a semantic animation engine—you still build the latter.

For this prototype, use Pixi’s ticker and your own small tween/event system. GSAP is optional, not necessary. Start with a fixed side-view camera and a few deliberate layer bands. Dynamic camera selection introduces another failure mode before you have solved contact correctness.

Do not start with a giant SVG library
Game Icons is available under CC BY 3.0, including commercial use with attribution. Iconify lists that set and its licence, but you must check the licence of whichever individual set you choose rather than treating “Iconify” as one blanket asset licence.

The engineering limitation is more important than search coverage: an icon is not an interactive prop.

A horse silhouette does not tell your system:

Where the saddle is.

Which way the horse faces.

Which leg should appear in front of the rider.

Whether it can animate.

Where reins should attach.

Prepare an offline asset manifest:

text
horse:
  rig_type: quadruped
  anchors: saddle, mouth, feet
  capabilities: rideable, locomotion
  layers: rear_legs, body, front_legs
For a tree, store trunk bounds, canopy region, ground contact, and optional branch anchors. For a chair, store seat and backrest geometry.

Automatic geometry analysis can propose bounding boxes, bases, and extrema. It cannot reliably infer semantic affordances from arbitrary SVG paths. Treat auto-anchors as offline authoring assistance requiring review, not as a solved runtime feature.

Start with perhaps 20–40 carefully prepared asset families, not thousands of unprepared icons. That number is a proposed scope, not a coverage estimate.

Use a consistent line weight, palette, character proportions, and ground treatment. Otherwise, the result will remain an icon collage even if everything moves.

Famous people
Use offline-made, owned or properly licensed heads on the same body rig.

A wig, beard, glasses, and hair-colour compositor is useful for generic people. It is not a reliable recognisable-person generator. Curated heads plus short name labels are more defensible.

Keep head artwork independent of the body so that a better artist can replace it without changing the motion system.

Rive, Spine, and Lottie
Rive runtimes are MIT-licensed; its editor and authoring arrangements are a separate consideration. Rive is worth considering if someone will author polished reusable character rigs. It still does not solve sentence interpretation or cross-object contacts.

For your first implementation:

Prefer direct PixiJS rigs.

Consider Rive later for authored character polish.

Do not make Spine a dependency while “zero cost” is a hard requirement without reviewing its specific editor/runtime terms.

Treat Lottie as a candidate for self-contained effects, not the core arbitrary-interaction system.

Those are architectural recommendations, not claims that these tools cannot support more elaborate integrations.

4. Motion retrieval and text-to-motion models
Retrieval is more appropriate than live generation—but optional
For this product, I would initially use authored procedural cycles and contact poses, not a large mocap database.

If you later add retrieval, do it this way:

Select and clean a small set of motions offline.

Retarget them to your rig offline.

Precompute labels or embeddings.

Retrieve locally.

Apply root placement, contact correction, and transitions at runtime.

A retrieved “sitting” motion does not know your chair height. A “riding” pose does not know your horse. Contact correction remains necessary.

Projecting 3D mocap directly into 2D also creates artistic problems: overlapping limbs, foreshortening, and motions that become unreadable from the chosen camera. It is a source of motion material, not an automatic stick-video style converter.

Model and dataset assessment

There is evidence that motion generation itself can be fast: Light-T2M reports 0.152 seconds versus 0.180 seconds for MoMask in its comparison. Those figures are paper-specific inference measurements, not measurements of your laptop, Whisper concurrency, model loading, rendering, or scene planning.

So the honest answer to “are these fast enough on 4 GB/CPU?” is:

Some models may fit and some may generate quickly, but I do not have evidence establishing your deadline on that hardware. None removes the main engineering problem, so they are a poor first dependency.

Keep code, checkpoint, dataset, body-model, and asset licences separate. An MIT model implementation does not grant rights to every dataset or checkpoint used with it.

5. Latency: your budget is already tight
Using your supplied estimates, the serial path is approximately:

Stage	Planning estimate
Final Whisper transcript	0.5–1.5 s, supplied by you
One Groq request	1–2 s, supplied by you; not a guaranteed service bound
Validation, local asset lookup, scene compilation	Target 10–100 ms; must benchmark
First displayed frame	Target next frame; must benchmark
Total	Roughly 1.5–3.6 s before additional tail delays
These are planning estimates, not measured performance results.

The 2–3 second target is plausible for typical cases, but not guaranteed. At the slow end of your own STT and LLM estimates, you already exceed it.

Possible improvements:

Prepare semantic interpretations from stable partial transcripts.

Commit only when the transcript is sufficiently stable.

Preload every live asset.

Send compact state deltas, not the full story.

Make exactly one inference call per committed beat.

Avoid mandatory repair calls.

Reject invalid output and retain the last correct scene.

Do not depend on live asset downloads.

Speculative partial-transcript processing helps latency, but creates correction complexity. Do not present it as free speed.

The free-tier token budget is a material blocker
Groq’s published free-plan table lists both gpt-oss-20b and gpt-oss-120b at 30 requests/minute, 8,000 tokens/minute, 1,000 requests/day, and 200,000 tokens/day. Exact organisation limits must be checked in account settings.

At ten sentences/minute:

2,000 uncached total tokens/call requires about 20,000 tokens/minute.

An 8,000-token/minute allowance permits about 800 tokens/call on average.

Therefore, “a few thousand tokens per line is OK” is not generally compatible with sustained sentence-by-sentence operation on that allowance.

Groq states cached tokens do not count toward rate limits, so stable prompt caching may help; it does not eliminate the need to measure actual account usage.

Build a token-budget test before investing in animation polish. A working five-line demo is not evidence of a working 40-minute lecture.

Frame rate is a separate metric
A smooth 60 fps renderer can still display an incorrect or late scene.

For a modest number of simple characters, 30–60 fps is a reasonable engineering target, including on integrated graphics. It is not established merely by “4 GB GPU”: CPU, browser, projector resolution, effects, and Whisper contention matter.

Measure with Whisper running and the projector connected.

6. Expected quality and your seven sentences
Expect a coherent animated diagram or modest stick-figure theatre—not a hand-directed YouTube film.

A good bounded implementation can provide:

Real walking and posture changes.

Correct attachment and object contact.

Persistent identities.

Consistent visual style.

Readable facial reactions and speaker bubbles.

Simple camera pans and scene transitions.

What it will lack is a human animator’s advance knowledge: staging future events, acting through pauses, choosing the most informative shot, varying gesture, and revising visual jokes. It cannot anticipate an unrevealed next sentence.

The following judgments assume the relevant assets and mechanics have been implemented; they are not measured success rates.

Sentence	Likely result	Required implementation / likely failure
“Newton was sitting under an apple tree.”	Good	Ground-sit pose, curated head, canopy-aware placement. Do not invent a chair. “Under” alone is insufficient.
“An apple fell on his head.”	Good with preceding context	Resolve “his”; preserve Newton’s position; spawn or identify an apple; choose a feasible fall path; trigger contact and reaction.
“Napoleon’s army crossed the Alps in winter.”	Schematic, not literal spectacle	Use a representative marching group, mountain route, and snow. Full armies traversing arbitrary terrain are outside the small prototype. Crossing requires a route, not merely soldiers beside mountains.
“Marie Curie worked day and night in a cold shed.”	Partial	Shed cutaway, curated Curie, day/night transition, cold cues. “Worked” does not specify the activity; avoid inventing a particular experiment as fact.
“The drop of water rises into the clouds.”	Good visually	Nonhuman entity following an upward path into a cloud region. As an educational choice, distinguish a personified water-drop story from a literal depiction of evaporation.
“The boy rode his horse to the village.”	Good only with a deliberately supported horse	Saddle attachment, rider pose, layered legs, horse locomotion, destination path. A retrieved horse SVG alone will not solve it.
“Einstein said to his friend, time is relative.”	Good	Two persistent characters, Einstein head, speaker-bound bubble, gaze toward friend. This depicts the supplied dialogue; it does not authenticate it as a historical quotation.
Likely weak cases include:

Ambiguous pronouns with several plausible referents.

Novel manipulable tools.

Dressing, tying knots, surgery, wrestling, and complex two-person contact.

Crowds interacting individually.

Arbitrary quadrupeds and unusual body plans.

Metaphorical verbs.

Nested interiors and difficult occlusion.

Scientific processes whose mechanics are not supplied by the sentence.

Long action chains arriving faster than they can be shown.

A water-drop journey and a blood-cell journey also need different world mechanics. You can reuse identities, paths, containment, and camera control, but not assume that a human motion library gives you biology or fluid-process coverage.

7. The smallest useful 1–2 day proof
Do not build a broad story demo. Build a relation-correctness test.

Day 1: remove the LLM entirely
Implement:

One attractive stick rig.

Tree, apple, chair, flask, and horse assets.

Ground-sit, chair-sit, hold, ride, look, and fall behaviours.

Named anchors.

Persistent world state.

JSON-driven events.

Feed hand-written semantic JSON into the engine.

Demonstrate:

A person sits under a tree.

An apple falls onto that person’s head.

The person holds a flask.

A rider stays attached while a horse moves.

The horse can have a simple deliberately authored cycle; it does not need naturalistic animation.

This isolates the failure that killed your previous attempt. If hand-written correct JSON still produces “beside” instead of “on,” the LLM is not the issue.

Day 2: add one constrained Groq call
Use the same runtime, then test unseen substitutions and paraphrases:

“A girl sat beneath the tree.”

“She picked up the flask.”

“The boy mounted the horse.”

“It carried him toward the village.”

“The apple fell beside him, not onto him.”

“He stood up but kept holding it.”

Include negative cases. A system that always attaches an apple to a head can pass the Newton demo while failing language understanding.

Suggested go/no-go gates:

Test	Proposed gate
Deterministic contacts with hand-written JSON	No visible detachment in tested poses
Local constraint assertions	Pass every supported test
Unseen supported paraphrases	At least 18/20 correct without per-sentence fixes
Unsupported action	Explicit fallback; no silently fabricated mechanic
Sentence-end to first correct visual	Measure median and p95; p95 ≤3 s if the deadline is truly hard
Continuous lecture-rate replay	No growing event queue or quota failure
Renderer under actual STT load	Stable 30 fps minimum
Visual acceptance	You actually accept the rig and four demonstrations
Those thresholds are proposed acceptance criteria, not published benchmarks.

A two-day proof can validate the architecture and visual direction. It cannot establish broad story coverage.

8. Existing projects: what is genuinely reusable?
Stick-Gen: relevant, but not proof of your requirement
gestura-ai/stick-gen is unusually close in stated scope. Its repository describes text-to-stick animation, structured script schemas, action mapping, motion export, and a decoupled browser-rendering path. It claims CPU inference around two seconds for its small variant and 60 fps playback of exported motion. Those are repository claims, not an independently verified result under your conditions.

Important caveats:

Its current licence is a custom Prosocial Public License variant, not straightforward MIT/Apache; the README describes revenue-sharing conditions for certain commercial users.

It reports use of AMASS and other motion datasets whose rights need separate examination.

Its advertised motion inference time does not establish continuous sentence-by-sentence scene editing, correct contacts, or end-to-end latency.

Potentially reusable material: schema design, skeleton conversion, motion-file format, and rendering concepts. Review the exact file/version licence before copying code.

I would inspect it, but I would not make it the foundation before reproducing your four contact tests.

Motion Canvas: useful animation infrastructure, not natural-language understanding
Motion Canvas is an MIT-licensed TypeScript animation system with a real-time editor, designed for informative vector animations synchronised with voice-overs.

It is relevant for deterministic effects and offline authoring. It is not an existing solution to unrestricted text → live, contact-correct character animation. Under your architecture, developers could write reusable animation primitives in it; the LLM would still emit only data.

What I would actually reuse
PixiJS for rendering.

Owned/properly licensed offline artwork.

A small custom rig and contact engine.

Motion Canvas ideas or components where they save implementation work.

Selected mocap only later, after licensing and contact correction are settled.

I did not find a demonstrated, drop-in project establishing all your requirements together.

Investment recommendation
Approve the two-day relation-first proof. Do not approve weeks of motion-model integration or mass SVG ingestion yet.

The viable direction is a bounded, extensible animation engine that accepts open language—not a general animation generator. Its decisive asset will be the quality of its capability contracts, contact constraints, and continuity handling. If that bounded product is unacceptable, the full requirement is not achievable reliably under the stated constraints.
