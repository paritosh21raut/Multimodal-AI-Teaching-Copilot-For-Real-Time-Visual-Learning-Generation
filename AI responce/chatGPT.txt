My honest assessment: build this, but change the definition of what "open-ended" means.

You can build a system that accepts previously unseen stories, identifies their entities, understands relationships, retrieves suitable visual assets, and constructs animated scenes without having a pre-made scene for every story.

You cannot realistically expect it to generate arbitrary, visually convincing animation with the freedom of a human animator, within 2–3 seconds, on a CPU-only or 4 GB GPU school laptop.

Those are two different engineering problems.

The most important thing: the bottleneck is not animation rendering. It is semantic grounding: turning language into a spatially correct, physically plausible scene.

A browser can easily animate hundreds of 2D objects at 60 FPS. Getting Newton underneath the tree, the apple attached to the tree before falling, and the apple actually contacting Newton's head is considerably harder.

1. The actual hard problem: semantic grounding, not animation
Your previous chibi experiment encountered the most important failure mode: it understood what objects existed, but failed to understand how those objects interacted.

A sentence contains more information than a collection of nouns and verbs.

Consider:
The boy rode his horse to the village.

A correct scene needs to understand:
- The boy is the rider.
- The horse is the vehicle.
- The boy must be positioned on the horse, not beside it.
- The boy's body must have a riding posture.
- His legs must relate to the horse's sides.
- His hands may connect to the reins.
- The horse moves along a path.
- The boy moves with the horse.
- The village is the destination.
A noun-and-verb parser cannot reliably produce all of this.

I would solve it with four interconnected systems.
A. Semantic scene graph
Your proposed scene graph is correct, but I would make one important modification.

Do not generate an entirely new scene for every sentence. Generate changes to a persistent scene state.

There are two layers:
1. World state: everything currently present and its relationships.
2. Event delta: what the new sentence changes.
For example:

Sentence 1: Newton was sitting under an apple tree.

The LLM returns something conceptually like:
{
  "entities": [
    {
      "id": "newton",
      "type": "person",
      "identity": "isaac_newton"
    },
    {
      "id": "tree_1",
      "type": "apple_tree"
    },
    {
      "id": "apple_1",
      "type": "apple"
    }
  ],
  "events": [
    {
      "actor": "newton",
      "action": "sit",
      "relation": {
        "type": "under",
        "target": "tree_1"
      }
    },
    {
      "actor": "apple_1",
      "action": "remain_attached",
      "target": "tree_1",
      "relation": "on_branch"
    }
  ]
}


This is illustrative, not a proposed final JSON schema.

Sentence 2: An apple fell on his head.

The LLM receives the existing state and returns a delta:
{
  "events": [
    {
      "actor": "apple_1",
      "action": "fall",
      "target": "newton",
      "interaction": "contact_head"
    },
    {
      "actor": "newton",
      "action": "react",
      "emotion": "surprised",
      "look_at": "apple_1"
    }
  ]
}


Notice what happened.

The LLM does not have to invent another Newton, another tree, or another apple. It resolves the reference to the existing apple and changes its state.

This is essential for pronouns, continuity, and multi-sentence narratives.
B. A typed spatial relationship system
This is where I would invest most of the engineering effort.

Do not treat spatial relations as descriptive labels. Treat them as geometric constraints.
Relationship	What the engine must calculate
Under	Place subject inside the target's lower spatial region
On	Align subject's contact point with target's support surface
Holding	Connect hand endpoint to object grip point
Riding	Align pelvis with saddle/seat and solve limb positions
In	Place subject inside a containment region
Next to	Place objects within a distance range without overlap
Above	Maintain vertical ordering with appropriate separation
Falling onto	Calculate trajectory and target collision
Looking at	Orient head and eyes toward the target
Pointing at	Solve arm and hand direction toward target

Every object should expose semantic anchors.

For example:
Example: horse asset









Saddle seat
Rider pelvis placement
Rein anchors
Rider hand placement
Left/right sides
Leg positioning
Ground contact
Horse positioning


The same principle applies to other objects.

An apple tree needs a trunk, canopy region, branch points and apple attachment points.

A table needs a support surface and leg geometry.

A chair needs a seat anchor and backrest.

A person's rig needs hand, foot, head and pelvis anchors.

My recommendation: create an explicit anchor metadata file for every important asset.

For example:
{
  "asset": "horse",
  "anchors": {
    "saddle_seat": [0.58, 0.34],
    "head": [0.91, 0.24],
    "ground": [0.50, 0.91],
    "left_rein": [0.89, 0.30]
  }
}


Coordinates here are normalized illustrative values.

Can anchors be extracted automatically from arbitrary SVG geometry? Partially.

You can extract bounding boxes, contours, extreme points and approximate surfaces. But software cannot reliably infer which horizontal line is a saddle, which part of a tree is a branch, or where an object should be gripped just from its silhouette.

That requires semantic annotations or a much more sophisticated vision system.

I would not spend weeks attempting fully automatic anchor detection. Build a small asset-authoring tool where you can mark anchors once and reuse them indefinitely.
C. Procedural character animation and inverse kinematics
I agree with your stick-figure/2D rig direction.

For the first implementation, use a skeleton with:
- Head
- Neck
- Torso
- Pelvis
- Upper arms
- Forearms
- Hands
- Thighs
- Lower legs
- Feet
Use inverse kinematics (IK) for the limbs.

IK allows you to specify the hand's destination, and the engine calculates the elbow position. The same principle works for feet and other joints.

You then build a small library of reusable motion primitives:
Primitive	Implementation
Idle	Breathing, blinking, subtle body movement
Walk	Procedural gait cycle
Run	Faster gait with body lean
Sit	Pelvis lowered, legs bent
Stand	Transition to standing pose
Point	Arm IK toward target
Hold	Hand IK constrained to object anchor
Ride	Pelvis constrained to saddle, riding gait
Fall	Gravity-driven motion
React	Head movement, body recoil, facial expression
Talk	Mouth movement and gestures

You do not need a separate animation for every sentence.

You need a reusable motion vocabulary that can be combined.

For instance:

walk + hold object + look at target

could generate a combination that was never explicitly authored.

That is compositional animation rather than scene templates.
D. A simple physics layer
Do not build a complete physics engine at the beginning.

You need only a small number of behaviours:
- Gravity
- Velocity
- Ground collision
- Object-to-object collision
- Contact detection
- Basic pushing and carrying
- Parent-child attachment and detachment
For Newton:
1. The apple initially belongs to a branch anchor.
2. The fall event detaches it.
3. Gravity determines its trajectory.
4. Collision detection identifies contact with Newton's head.
5. The engine switches Newton's pose to surprised/recoiling.
6. The apple settles or falls to the ground.
That is a small deterministic physics problem, not an AI motion-generation problem.

3. Motion-generation research: should you use T2M-GPT, MoMask or MDM?
This is an important distinction: text-to-motion models generate human joint movements. They do not generate a complete story scene.

They can tell a skeleton how to walk, bend or jump. They do not inherently know that a person must sit on a particular horse, that the horse must carry the person, or that an apple must collide with someone's head.

5. Expected latency on ordinary school hardware
There are two separate performance questions.

A. Can the animation run at 30–60 FPS?

Yes. With a reasonably small 2D scene, cached assets and procedural animation, this is not a particularly difficult computational problem.

B. Can the complete understanding-to-animation pipeline finish in 2–3 seconds?

This is much more challenging.

Based on the latencies you supplied:
Operation	Expected time
Whisper sentence finalization	0.5–1.5 s
Groq request and response	1–2 s
JSON validation	1–10 ms
Asset resolution	1–20 ms
Layout and constraint solving	1–20 ms
Initial render/update	10–100 ms

These are engineering estimates for the local stages, not benchmark results from your implementation.
Estimated end-to-end delay
1.5–3.6 s
Calculated from your supplied Whisper and Groq ranges plus small local processing overhead.

My conclusion

The 2–3-second target is attainable in favourable conditions, but cannot be promised consistently with this pipeline.

There are several practical optimizations.

1. Do not wait for a perfect sentence-final transcript.

Use partial Whisper transcripts.

As soon as the emerging sentence contains enough information to identify an event, start a provisional interpretation. Update the scene once the final transcription arrives.

For example, when the teacher says:

"Newton was sitting..."

You can identify the character and sitting action before receiving the remainder.

When "...under an apple tree" arrives, add the missing object and spatial constraint.

This is much better than waiting for every sentence to finish before beginning all processing.

2. Keep the LLM response small.

You do not need several thousand tokens per line.

A compact event delta with a few entities and relationships should be enough. The previous scene state can be summarized and supplied as context.

3. Preload your asset library.

No asset search over the internet should occur during classroom operation.

Use a local indexed registry with synonyms and categories.

4. Keep animation independent of the LLM.

The animation must continue while the next sentence is being understood.

Do not stop the entire scene every time an API call is pending.

5. Build a fallback.

Groq has model-specific request and token limits, and the applicable limits can depend on the account's plan. A free service is not a hard real-time infrastructure guarantee. 
GroqDocs+1


Your renderer should continue working when an LLM request fails, times out or is rate-limited.
Which Groq model?
I would start with openai/gpt-oss-20b.

Current Groq documentation lists GPT-OSS 20B among models supporting strict structured outputs. With strict JSON-schema mode, output schema adherence is guaranteed. 
GroqDocs+1


There is a catch: Groq documents that structured-output mode does not support streaming. 
GroqDocs


For the first prototype, I would favour strict schema validation over token streaming. The JSON response should be small enough that waiting for its completion is reasonable.

For a later latency experiment, compare strict structured output against JSON-object mode with your own validator.

7. Judging your seven example sentences
Here is how I would assess the actual engineering difficulty.
Sentence	Feasibility	Main challenge
Newton was sitting under an apple tree.	High	Sitting pose, ground placement, tree canopy
An apple fell on his head.	High	Continuity, gravity, head collision, reaction
Napoleon's army crossed the Alps in winter.	Medium	Multiple characters, terrain, crowd movement

The drop of water rises into the clouds.	High	Non-human animation, evaporation, trajectory
The boy rode his horse to the village.	High with authored horse asset	Rider-to-saddle attachment, horse gait
Einstein said to his friend, time is relative.	High with identity assets	Speaker identification, dialogue and gaze

Some important qualifications:

Newton: This is one of your best initial test cases. You need a tree with semantic regions, a person rig and a sitting animation.

Apple: This is an excellent test of state continuity. The apple should exist in the first scene and become the same object in the second. An isolated sentence is easier to represent, but continuing the previous scene is the real test.

Napoleon: This is not simply a question of generating an army. You need a crowd system. Do not create 100 independently simulated, LLM-controlled people. Generate perhaps 8–12 visible soldier figures, with instanced or grouped movement representing a larger army. Use a mountain environment, snow effects and a left-to-right crossing path.

Marie Curie: The phrase "day and night" is a temporal instruction. The environment can transition from daylight to moonlight while the character continues working. The shed, clothing, desk and laboratory objects should be asset selections. Cold could be communicated through snow outside, shivering and visible breath.

Water drop: This is a useful test because your engine must support non-humanoid agents. A droplet needs a different rig from a human. Its rising movement can be procedural, with small vapour particles and clouds.

Horse: This is perhaps the most important relational test. The rider must remain attached to the saddle while the horse moves. The two must animate as a coordinated system.

Einstein: Dialogue should be assigned to the character, not automatically to the teacher. The character should face his friend, and the speech bubble should originate from the correct speaker. This also tests quotation and discourse understanding.

I would make the horse and apple examples mandatory acceptance tests. They directly expose the failure in your previous implementation.

10. My final engineering recommendation
I would make one major architectural decision before investing weeks:

Do not make this a text-to-animation-generation project. Make it a language-driven procedural scene-composition engine.

The distinction is fundamental.
Text-to-animation approach	Procedural scene-composition approach
Generates movement from text	Extracts events and relationships
Relies on learned motion patterns	Uses deterministic movement primitives
May generate visually plausible but incorrect interactions	Explicitly solves interaction constraints
Model inference in the critical path	Only language understanding requires an LLM
Harder to guarantee repeatability	Deterministic and debuggable

1. Build the spatial relationship engine.

2. Build the reusable character rig.

GO
 for a constrained, compositional, real-time 2D scene engine.

NO-GO
